# Evidence map: rampwise-strength

Source names refer to `library.md` in this folder. "Verified" = read in the abstract or full text (2026-11-16).
Strength: **research** = meta-analysis/review; **practice** = convention without a study in this library; **gap** = unsupported.

| Rule or advice | What the source says (verified) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength training improves endurance performance | Berryman 2018 (meta-analysis, 28 studies; running, cycling, skiing, swimming): net SMD 0.52 (0.33-0.70) for middle/long-distance performance; energy cost of locomotion 0.65 (0.32-0.98), maximal force 0.99, maximal power 0.50; maximal-force training led to greater improvements than other intensities; effects consistent irrespective of athlete level. Blagrove 2018 (systematic review, 24 studies of runners; full text): running economy improved 2-8 % in most studies; time-trial (1.5-10 km) performance tended to improve; VO2max, lactate and body composition usually unaffected; "two to three ST sessions per week ... with a variety of modalities are likely to provide benefits". | research | Full text of Blagrove in `papers/` |
| Heavy strength for cycling; heavy or explosive for running economy | Ronnestad and Mujika 2014 (review, abstract): running economy improved by heavy or explosive strength; heavy strength recommended for cycling economy; effects on lactate-threshold power equivocal; most compelling evidence of an additive effect on cycling performance with heavy strength. | research (review) | |
| Strength reduces injuries | Lauersen 2014 (meta-analysis of 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants, 3,464 injuries): strength training RR 0.315 (0.207-0.480), proprioception 0.550, multiple exposures 0.655, stretching no benefit (0.963); acute RR 0.647, overuse RR 0.527. | research (meta-analysis, mixed sports) | Not runner/cyclist-specific; says nothing about which exercises |
| Concurrent training barely affects maximal strength or size, may blunt explosive strength | Schumann 2022 (meta-analysis, 43 studies; full text): SMD -0.06 maximal strength (ns), -0.28 explosive strength (p=0.007), -0.01 hypertrophy (ns). | research | |
| Separate strength from cardio by at least 3 h | Schumann 2022: explosive-strength attenuation more pronounced when done in the same session (<= 20 min between; SMD -0.31, p=0.043) than when separated by at least 3 h (p > 0.05); no significant moderators for cycling vs running, >5 vs <5 weekly sessions, training status, age. Blagrove 2018 recommends > 3 h after high-intensity running before strength, noting most studies used different days or gaps over 3 h. | research (meta-analysis + review recommendation) | The 3 h figure is a subgroup threshold, not a proven minimum |
| If combined in one session, strength first | Eddens 2018 (meta-analysis of 10 studies, >= 5 weeks; full text): resistance-then-endurance favoured lower-body dynamic strength (weighted mean difference 6.91 %, 95 % CI 1.96-11.87, p=0.006); no effect of order on hypertrophy, static strength, aerobic capacity or body fat. | research (small number of studies) | |
| Interference grows with endurance modality, frequency, duration | Wilson 2012 (meta-analysis, 21 studies): concurrent training with running, not cycling, produced significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength; endurance frequency and duration correlated negatively with hypertrophy, strength and power. | research (older meta-analysis) | Partly at odds with Schumann 2022 (no significant running vs cycling difference) |
| Evening session depends on recovery | Craven 2022 (see recovery evidence map): sleep loss mainly impaired afternoon/evening tasks, morning tasks largely unaffected. | research | Used to gate evening strength on sleep and readiness |
| No heavy lower-body work the evening before a key session | No verified paper quantifies next-day effects on endurance sessions. | practice | Flagged as context only |
| Strength frequency | Blagrove 2018: two to three sessions per week for runners. No verified frequency for cyclists or for athletes already training 5-6 days. | research for runners; gap otherwise | |
| Injury-resistance exercise selection (single-leg, hips, calves, trunk) | Not tested in the papers read. | practice | |

| Sprint speed rises with lower-body strength (squat) | Seitz 2014 (abstract): r = -0.77 between squat-strength effect size and sprint effect size across 15 studies; frequency per week and rest between sets moderated it; number of exercises per session, sets per exercise and %1RM did not. | research (meta-analysis) | Mostly young team-sport athletes; an association across studies, not a promise for one person |
| How sprinters train strength | Haugen 2019 (full text): strength and power 2-3 times a week in preparation; sprint-specific lifts (split squats, single-leg deadlifts, lunges, step-ups); strength the day after sprint-specific training; ballistic loads up to about 60% 1RM for power, heavier for force. | research (expert synthesis, not a trial) | Elite sprinters; the science-practice gap is stated in the paper |
| Hamstrings: horizontal force and injuries | Morin 2015: horizontal force went with pre-contact hamstring activation and eccentric hamstring strength (n = 14). van Dyk 2019 (abstract): Nordic hamstring programmes, risk ratio 0.49 (0.32-0.74), 15 studies. Cuthbert 2020: lower Nordic volumes gave similar strength and fascicle gains. | research | Team-sport athletes; Nordics need an anchor (library: anchor_feet) |
| Plyometrics and sprint | Saez de Villarreal 2012 (abstract): mixed jump types, horizontal jumps, at least 15 sessions, more than 80 combined jumps per session were most likely to improve sprint; no extra benefit from added weight. Li 2025: resisted sprint training SMD 0.65 for linear sprint, strongest for 0-10 m. | research | No paper read gives a safe jump progression for beginners: the 20-30 contact start is practice |
| Weekly volume and strength | Ralston 2017: more weekly sets, more strength (ES 0.18, high vs low). Schoenfeld 2019: 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise (3 sessions a week, 8 weeks, trained men) gave similar strength; muscle size rose with volume. Iversen 2021: at least 4 weekly sets per muscle group in a 6-15 RM range for time-efficient training. | research | Volume above the minimum raises hypertrophy more than strength; no verified ceiling for athletes who also do endurance training |
| Load for strength vs size | Lopez 2021 (network meta-analysis): hypertrophy similar across loads when sets go near failure; strength gains larger with loads of 80% 1RM or more (up to 8RM). Refalo 2023: training to failure was not better than stopping short for hypertrophy. | research | Home equipment may not allow 80% 1RM: slow tempo, pauses and single-leg work are practice |
| Frequency | Grgic 2018 (abstract): more sessions a week gave more strength, but not when volume was equal. Cuthbert 2021: no clear difference between frequencies in well-trained people with volume equated. | research | Lets sessions be shortened or split without losing the effect, if total sets stay equal |
| Rest between sets | Grgic 2018 (abstract): at least 2 minutes advised for resistance-trained people, 60-120 s for untrained. McLeod 2024 umbrella review: inter-set rest did not appear to affect strength or mass. | research (mixed) | The plans use 90-150 s |
| Unilateral vs bilateral | Liao 2022: unilateral training helped unilateral jumping, bilateral training helped bilateral strength; no difference for sprint speed (ES -0.12) or change of direction. | research (meta-analysis) | |
| Runners: economy | Balsalobre-Fernandez 2016 (abstract): strength training (2-4 lower-body exercises, up to 200 jumps, 5-10 short sprints, 2-3 times a week, 8-12 weeks) improved running economy in highly trained middle- and long-distance runners (SMD -1.42, 5 trials, 93 runners). Eihara 2022: heavy resistance training (g -0.32) beat plyometrics (g -0.13), best with loads of 90% 1RM or more. Llanos-Lagos 2024: high-load and combined methods improved running performance; none improved VO2max, vVO2max, steady state or sprint capacity. | research | Small trials, trained runners; certainty very low to moderate |
| Runners: injuries | Toresdahl 2020 (RCT, 720 first-time marathoners): a 10-minute quadriceps, hip and core programme 3 times a week did not reduce marathon-stopping overuse injury (7.1% vs 7.3%). Mucha 2017: hip-abductor weakness linked only to iliotibial band syndrome. Ferrauti 2010: 2 strength sessions a week raised knee-extension force; no clear change in running economy. | research | So no injury-prevention claim is made for a general strength routine |
| Ultra and trail | Giovanelli 2017 (RCT, 25 ultra-marathoners): 12 weeks of home-based strength, explosive and plyometric training lowered the cost of running 3-6%; at least 3 sessions a week advised. | research (small RCT) | Eccentric / downhill-specific work: gap (no trial read) |
| Cyclists: road performance | Ronnestad 2010 (RCT): 4 lower-body exercises, 3 x 4-10 RM, twice a week for 12 weeks raised thigh size, force, Wingate power and 40-minute trial power. Ronnestad 2017: 10 weeks in elite cyclists raised Wingate power; 40-minute trial not significantly different. Yamamoto 2010: gains came when strength replaced part of endurance training; adding it on top did not help in 2 of 5 studies. | research | Small samples; trials in well-trained and elite men mostly |
| Cyclists: sprint | Stone 2004 (abstract): maximum isometric strength correlated with Wingate power, coach's rank and standing-start sprint times in 20-30 sprint cyclists. | research (correlation) | No cycling-specific jump trial read |
| Cyclists: bone | Mathis 2018 (abstract): weight training was the only predictor of higher spine and hip bone density in 40 competitive male cyclists. | research (cross-sectional) | Association; no trial read on jumps or lifting for bone in cyclists: gap |
| Triathletes | Luckin-Baldwin 2021 (RCT, 25 triathletes): 26 weeks moving from 8-12 reps (up to 75% 1RM) to 1-6 reps (85% 1RM or more) improved cycling and running economy. | research | Single trial |
| Goal profiles, patterns, slot order, 2-4 weekly sets per pattern, jump contacts | The profile files cite which numbers are research and which are practice. | practice | See references/goal_profiles.json |
| Garmin stores sets, reps and weight when a strength workout is logged | Checked on one user's own FIT files (set messages: exercise category and name, repetitions, weight, duration, rest). Sessions without a structured workout have only time and heart rate. | fact (verified by reading the files) | weight units and per-hand vs total are not stated by Garmin: verify with the user |

## Not supported (do not claim)
- Specific sets, reps, loads or weekly lower-body volume for concurrent athletes.
- That order or timing changes endurance performance (the studies measured strength outcomes).
- Next-day interference numbers.
- That strength training prevents any particular injury in runners/cyclists (evidence is for sports injuries overall).
