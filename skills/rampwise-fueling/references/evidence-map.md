# Evidence map: rampwise-fueling

Source names refer to `library.md` in this folder. "Verified" = read in the abstract or full text (2026-11-16).
Strength: **research** = review/consensus/trial; **practice** = convention without a study in this library; **gap** = unsupported.

| Rule used by the scripts | What the source says (verified) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duration tiers for carbohydrate | Jeukendrup 2014 (Sports Med; full text): about 1 h: mouth rinse or small amounts of carbohydrate can improve performance; ACSM guideline (cited in the paper): 30-60 g/h over 1 h; a single source oxidises up to about 60 g/h, recommended for 2-3 h; ultra-endurance: about 90 g/h, from multiple transportable carbohydrates; multiple-carb benefit seen mostly in studies of 2.5 h or longer. | research (review/guideline) | Basis of `fuel_plan.py` |
| Absolute g/h, not per kg | Same paper: no correlation between body weight and exogenous carbohydrate oxidation; limiting factor is absorption. | research | |
| Lower the amount at low absolute intensity | Same paper: "adjusted (downwards)"; no factor given; example of a 5-h marathoner who may not need 90 g/h. | research (qualitative) | No multiplier applied |
| Dose-response and the 60-80 g/h optimum | Same paper describes Smith et al.: 51 cyclists/triathletes, 2-h ride at moderate-high intensity then 20-km time trial, 12 glucose:fructose doses from 10-120 g/h; performance improved dose-dependently, greatest at 60-80 g/h. | research (multicentre trial, described in a review) | |
| Cap at 90 g/h | Podlogar and Wallis 2022 (narrative review; full text): recommended maximum up to 90 g/h; glucose+fructose mixtures advised above 2.5 h and 60-90 g/h; 120 g/h gave higher exogenous oxidation in highly trained athletes but performance benefit is unclear and "at present it cannot be recommended to alter the currently recommended maximal carbohydrate ingestion dose". | research (review) | Commercial pages quoting 100-120 g/h are not supported as a general recommendation |
| Practise the plan / gut training | Jeukendrup 2014: gut adaptation shown in animals and one human study (28 days of high carbohydrate intake raised exogenous oxidation); limited human evidence, but practising is "highly recommended". Jeukendrup 2017 (periodised nutrition review; full text): adaptations include better intestinal absorptive capacity and tolerance to dehydration; some methods well supported, others theory. | research (limited) | The +10 g/h per week ramp is **practice**, not from a paper |
| Fluid: customise; avoid > 2 % body-mass loss | ACSM position stand 2007 (abstract): goal of drinking is to prevent excessive (> 2 % body weight loss from water deficit) dehydration and excessive electrolyte change; individual sweat rates vary, so customised plans; sweat rate can be estimated from body weight before and after; electrolyte/carbohydrate drinks can help over water alone in some circumstances. | research (position stand) | Basis of `sweat_rate.py` |
| Solid food and concentrated drinks reduce fluid absorption | Jeukendrup 2014 (full text). | research (review) | |
| Late-session fade is not only fuel | Grivas 2026 (narrative review): wall/late collapse emerges from metabolic, thermoregulatory, dehydration, GI, neuromuscular, perceptual and pacing factors. Wang 2026: decoupling onset related to core-temperature rise and sodium loss per km; late decoupling related to glucose change (13 adolescent runners). | research (review + small study) | |
| General sports-nutrition framework | Thomas, Erdman and Burke 2016 (ACSM/AND/DC position, abstract only): performance and recovery enhanced by well-chosen nutrition; refer athletes to a registered dietitian for personalised plans. | research (position) | Basis for referral wording |
| Hyponatraemia and sodium guidance | Hew-Butler 2015 consensus statement is in PubMed **without an abstract** and no full text was available. | gap | No sodium numbers are given anywhere |

## Not supported (do not claim)
- Sodium or electrolyte targets (g/L, mg/h).
- 100-120 g/h as a general recommendation.
- A multiplier for low intensity.
- That the +10 g/h per week ramp is a studied protocol.
- Fuelling advice for low energy availability or medical conditions: refer (the meal plan stops at the gates below).

## Fuelling and hydration across sports (read 2026-11-17)
- **Jeukendrup 2011 (review of nutrition for marathon, triathlon and road cycling; abstract read, paywalled).** For efforts of 30 minutes or more, dehydration and carbohydrate depletion are the likely causes of fatigue; gastrointestinal problems, hyperthermia and hyponatraemia can hurt performance and be health threatening, especially in events over about 4 hours; the carbohydrate plan should be individual and depend on absolute intensity and duration; limit body-mass loss through sweating to 2-3 %; gut problems are frequent and individual and may relate to concentrated carbohydrate drinks, hyperosmotic drinks, fibre, fat and protein; hyponatraemia appears mainly in slower competitors who drink a lot of water or low-sodium drinks. **So:** supports individual, practised plans and not drinking "ahead" of thirst or sweat loss; research (review, abstract level).
- **ACSM 2007 fluid position stand (abstract read).** Start euhydrated; during exercise prevent more than 2 % body-weight loss from water deficit and large electrolyte shifts; sweat rates vary a lot, so customise, using body weight before and after to estimate sweat rate. **So:** the sweat-rate script follows this; research (position stand).
- **Mougin 2025 (abstract read):** carbohydrate use is higher in the heat and when dehydrated (see the heat map).
- **Guevara 2024 (full text read; see the load-injury map):** gastrointestinal and cardiac problems in short-course triathlon were attributed mainly to contaminated water and thermal strain: swim-water quality and heat are fuelling and illness risks for triathletes, not only a food question.
- **Gaps (no source read):** triathlon-specific carbohydrate targets (bike versus run), fuelling for swims (pool or open water), duathlon, and any sodium target. The skill keeps its existing rule: no sodium numbers; practise the plan in training.

## Daily nutrition: cutting, bulking and meal plans (read 2026-11-18)
Everything below is what `nutrition_targets.py`, `meal_plan.py` and `weight_trend.py` use. Papers marked full text were read in `papers/`; abstract-only ones are capped at what the abstract says.

| Rule used by the scripts | What the source says (verified) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resting energy = Mifflin-St Jeor: 10 x kg + 6.25 x cm - 5 x age + 5 (men) or - 161 (women) | Mifflin 1990 (abstract): derived from 498 healthy adults (19-78 y), measured by indirect calorimetry; Harris-Benedict overestimated by 5 %. Frankenfield 2005 (systematic review, abstract): Mifflin-St Jeor predicted within 10 % of measured in more people than the other common equations, "but noteworthy errors and limitations exist when it is applied to individuals". | research (derivation + review) | Athletes were not the study group; an estimate to be corrected by the weight trend |
| Non-training activity factor 1.2 / 1.3 / 1.45 and 8 kcal per kg per training hour (fallback) | No source read. | practice | Replace the fallback with the watch's own kcal per hour when known (`--kcal-per-hour`); the trend corrects the rest |
| 7700 kcal per kg of body weight to turn a % per week into kcal | Helms 2014 (full text) quotes about 3500 kcal per pound of fat. Hall 2011 (abstract only): body-weight response to a change in intake is slow and energy expenditure adapts, so a fixed figure drifts over time. | practice (rule of thumb, adaptation noted) | Why `weight_trend.py` exists |
| Cut rate 0.5 %/week default, 0.5-1 % allowed, warn above 0.7 % | Helms 2014 (full text, review): about 0.5-1 % of body weight per week to maximise muscle retention; diets of 2-4 months. Garthe 2011 (abstract; 24 athletes, randomised): 0.7 %/week (intake cut by 19 %) gained lean mass (+2.1 %) and strength, 1.4 %/week (intake cut by 30 %) did not; fat loss 31 % versus 21 %. | research (review + small RCT) | Small sample; athletes who also did resistance training, not endurance-only |
| Deficit capped at 25 % of estimated maintenance | The two Garthe arms cut intake by 19 % and 30 %. | practice (anchored on that trial) | |
| Cut length about 12 weeks, then 2-4 weeks at maintenance | Helms 2014: 2-4 months of dieting. Byrne 2018 (MATADOR, abstract read; 51 men with obesity): alternating 2-week blocks of energy restriction and energy balance gave more weight and fat loss than continuous restriction. | research for the length (review); the break is practice | The diet-break trial was in men with obesity, not athletes |
| Protein on a cut: 2.3 g per kg fat-free mass, or 2.0 g per kg body mass when body fat is unknown | Helms 2014 and Jager 2017 (full text): 2.3-3.1 g/kg lean mass in resistance-trained people during hypocaloric periods; Helms cites 1.8-2.7 g/kg body mass for athletes in energy deficit. | research (review, position stand) | Low end used; the sources allow higher |
| Protein maintaining 1.6 g/kg; bulking 1.8 g/kg | Morton 2018 (meta-analysis, 49 trials, 1,863 people): no further gain in fat-free mass above about 1.62 g/kg/day. Jager 2017: 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day for most exercisers. Iraki 2019: 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day off-season. | research | Resistance-training outcomes; for endurance-only athletes a reasonable, not a proven, figure |
| Protein per meal and spread | Schoenfeld and Aragon 2018 (full text): 0.4 g/kg/meal over at least four meals to reach 1.6 g/kg (0.55 g/kg/meal at 2.2 g/kg/day). Iraki 2019: 0.40-0.55 g/kg per meal, 3-6 meals. Jager 2017: 0.25 g/kg or 20-40 g per dose, every 3-4 h. Helms 2014: moderate differences in meal frequency (3-6 meals) have negligible effect. | research (reviews) | The planner spreads protein evenly over 3-6 meals; it does not claim an optimal number of meals |
| Fat 20-30 % of energy on a cut (35 % max otherwise), never under 0.5 g/kg or 20 % | Helms 2014: 15-30 % of calories on a cut. Slater 2019 (full text): ACSM advises 20-35 %; athletes should be discouraged from intakes below 15-20 %. Iraki 2019: 0.5-1.5 g/kg/day. | research (reviews) | The 20 % floor is the higher end of the cautions |
| Carbohydrate = the remainder, with a daily floor by training day | Kerksick 2018 (full text): 3-5 g/kg/day for general fitness; 5-8 g/kg/day for 2-3 h of intense training 5-6 days a week; 8-10 g/kg/day for 3-6 h a day. Kerksick 2017 (full text): glycogen is maximised at 8-12 g/kg/day. Iraki 2019: at least 3-5 g/kg for resistance training. | research for the bands | Mapping our day types (rest, under 1 h, 1-2 h, 2-3 h, over 3 h) onto the bands, and the floors 3 / 3 / 4 / 5 / 6 g/kg, is **practice** |
| Hard days keep their carbohydrate; the deficit moves to easier days | Helms 2014: below a practical carbohydrate threshold performance and lean mass suffer; when leaner, reduce the deficit by adding carbohydrate (25-50 g = 100-200 kcal). IOC REDs 2023 (abstract): low carbohydrate availability is an emerging factor. | practice built on research | |
| Bulk surplus about 10-20 % (20 / 15 / 10 % for novice / intermediate / advanced), gain 0.25-0.5 %/week | Iraki 2019 (full text, narrative review): about 10-20 % above maintenance, 0.25-0.5 % of body weight a week; advanced lifters should be more conservative; a 200-300 kcal surplus may suit highly trained athletes better than 500 (it cites an elite-athlete trial). Slater 2019: no controlled study has found the best surplus. | research (review), weak | Which of 10, 15, 20 % by level is practice |
| Weight-trend rule: 7-day averages, wait two weeks, change by 100-200 kcal | Iraki 2019: average weekly weight from several weigh-ins; adjust energy by rate of gain. Helms 2014: 100-200 kcal steps. Two weeks and the exact thresholds are practice. | practice built on research | |
| Energy availability check when body fat is known: warn under 30 kcal per kg fat-free mass | Jeukendrup 2024 (full text): about 45 kcal/kg FFM/day is generally considered healthy and below 30 problematic, from two short studies in sedentary women; the IOC 2023 consensus says energy availability cannot be measured accurately enough in the field; REDs symptoms are generic and have many possible causes. | research (critical review), weak | A conservative guard, not a diagnosis. The scripts never claim a person has REDs |
| Gates: under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, eating-disorder history or concern, diet-relevant medical condition, BMI under 20 or very low body fat on a cut: no plan | Helms 2014: physique athletes have a higher risk of eating and body-image disorders; leaner athletes lose more muscle when cutting; IOC 2023 (abstract) links mental health and REDs. Thomas 2016 (abstract): refer athletes to a registered dietitian for a personalised plan. The specific cut-offs (BMI 20; body fat 10 % men, 17 % women) are **practice**. | research for the direction, practice for the thresholds | |
| Meal timing around training | Kerksick 2017 (full text): carbohydrate 1-4 g/kg in the hours before higher-intensity, longer exercise; protein 0.25-0.40 g/kg per dose; post-exercise protein within about 2 h; 1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate when recovery is under 4 h. | research (position stand) | |
| Food list | USDA FoodData Central SR Legacy (public domain): nutrient values per 100 g, each food keeps its USDA ID. Southeast Asian (Bahasa) aliases (tempe, tahu, nasi, ubi, singkong, bandeng...) only help search; Southeast Asian-style meals are approximations built from basic ingredients, not restaurant dishes. | data | Recipes, brands and cooking change values; add your own with `foods.py add` |

## Race-day fuelling timeline (read 2026-11-18)
| Rule used by `race_day.py` | What the source says (verified) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brief taper with at least 8 g/kg/day carbohydrate before a long hard effort | Kerksick 2017 (full text): glycogen strategies should start with a short reduction in training and a high daily carbohydrate intake (at least 8 g/kg/day). | research (position stand) | Two to three days is practice |
| Main meal 3-4 h before, 1-4 g/kg carbohydrate | Kerksick 2017: 1-4 g/kg in the hours before higher-intensity (70 % VO2max or more), longer (over 90 min) exercise; a meal 4 h before raised muscle and liver glycogen in one study; solid or liquid carbohydrate restored glycogen similarly. Jeukendrup 2011 (abstract): gut problems may relate to fibre, fat, protein and concentrated drinks. | research | The 2 g/kg default and the low fat/fibre advice are practice |
| Food 60 min or more before is fine; the last hour is the delicate part | Kerksick 2017: carbohydrate fed under 60 min before exercise caused a blood-sugar dip in an early study; a later study found the response negated by a proper warm-up, and moving the intake closer to the start (15 min versus 75 min) lowered the chance of symptoms; studies feeding 60 min or more before found no harm, some found 7-20 % better performance; a 6.4 % carbohydrate drink 30 min before beat 120 min before in one trial. | research (review of small studies) | Individual response: practise it |
| Take carbohydrate at regular intervals, g/h by duration | Kerksick 2017: 230-350 mL of a 6-8 % solution every 10-12 min; 30-60 g/h. Jeukendrup 2014 and Podlogar 2022 for the tiers and the 90 g/h cap (see above). | research | The 15 min default interval and starting at minute 15 are practice |
| Recovery within 4 h: 1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate, or 0.8 g/kg/h with 0.2-0.4 g/kg/h protein | Kerksick 2017 abstract and text. | research | |
| **User question: does a late meal or gel take a long time to give a boost?** | **No source read gives a time delay.** What was read: a large carbohydrate dose in the last 30-60 min before the start can cause a blood-sugar dip in some athletes (individual, reduced by warm-up); carbohydrate taken during the effort maintains blood glucose (Kerksick 2017); the limit on carbohydrate use during exercise is delivery to the muscle, about 60 g/h from one source and up to 90 g/h from several (Jeukendrup 2014, Podlogar 2022). | research for the parts read; **gap** for any minutes-to-effect figure | The plan therefore says: eat the main meal early, keep the last hour simple, start taking carbohydrate early and regularly in the race |
| Triathlon bike-to-run fuelling, swim fuelling | No source read. | gap | General long-session guidance only |

## Not supported (added with the nutrition scripts)
- Any claim that a particular macro split, meal frequency or meal time is optimal for body composition beyond the ranges above.
- Sodium or electrolyte targets, peak-week dehydration or "water cutting" (Helms 2014 calls dehydration and electrolyte manipulation dangerous): never planned.
- A fixed number of kcal per kg that always turns into fat or muscle; the 7700 figure is a starting estimate.
- A plan for under-18s, pregnancy or breastfeeding, an eating-disorder history or concern, or a medical condition: referred.
- Supplements (creatine, caffeine, beta-alanine): Helms 2014 and Iraki 2019 discuss them but the scripts give no dose; the planner only uses whey or soy protein powder as an ordinary protein food.
- Nutrient adequacy beyond energy and the three macros (iron, calcium, vitamin D...): not checked; a dietitian can do it.
