# Evidence map: rampwise-load-injury

Source names refer to `library.md` in this folder. "Verified" = read in the abstract or full text (2026-11-16).
Strength: **research** = trial/cohort/meta-analysis/consensus; **practice** = convention without a study in this library; **gap** = unsupported.

| Rule used by `load_check.py` | What the source says (verified) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| No single run more than 10 % longer than the longest run of the last 30 days | Frandsen 2025 (BJSM; 5,205 adult runners, 18-month cohort, 588,071 sessions, distance data from Garmin devices; 1,820 injuries): relative to up to 10 % increase, injury rates were higher for small spikes >10-30 % (HRR 1.64, 95 % CI 1.31-2.05), moderate >30-100 % (HRR 1.52, 1.16-2.00) and large >100 % (HRR 2.28, 1.50-3.48) in single-session distance. Conclusion: higher rate of overuse injury when a session exceeded 10 % of the longest run in the last 30 days. Week-to-week ratio: no relationship; ACWR: negative dose-response. Self-reported injuries. | research (large observational) | Full text in `papers/` (CC BY-NC). Association, not proof of cause; adults mostly 45 +/- 10 y |
| Weekly distance jumps above ~30 % are a caution | Nielsen 2014 (874 novice runners, 1 year, GPS): rates did not differ significantly across groups (<10 %, 10-30 %, >30 %); distance-related injuries were more frequent with >30 % (HR 1.59, 95 % CI 0.96-2.66, P=.07). Damsted 2019 (261 runners, 14 weeks, half-marathon preparation): at 21 days more runners were injured after 20-60 % weekly increases (risk difference 22.6 %, 95 % CI 0.9-44.3 %), no significant difference after 56 and 98 days. Damsted 2018 (systematic review, 4 articles): "very limited evidence" that sudden load change is associated with injury. | research (observational; limited) | Used as NOTE only |
| A graded "10 % rule" plan is not proven protective | Buist 2008 (RCT, 532 novice runners, 13-week graded programme based on the 10 % rule vs 8-week standard): injury incidence 20.8 % vs 20.3 %, no effect. | research (RCT, novices) | Why weekly 10 % is not enforced |

## Not supported (do not claim)
- That staying under 10 % per week prevents injury (the RCT found no effect).
- A pass/fail ACWR band.
- Any numeric rule for rides, for strength, or for double sessions.
- That the numbers apply to injury types other than running-related overuse in the studied populations (adult recreational, novice or mixed).

## Full-text reading notes (read in full 2026-11-17)
What the full texts add to the rows above: sample, effect, and the limits the authors state. Where a row above says more than this, this section wins.

- **Frandsen 2025 (BJSM; full text read).** Design: the Garmin-RUNSAFE prospective cohort, 18 months, 5,205 adult runners (mean age 45.8, 22 % women, median 9.5 years of running), recruited through Garmin newsletters and European running clubs; distance came from Garmin devices, injuries from a weekly self-report; 1,820 runners (35 %) reported an injury, 1,311 of them overuse. Single-session result (reference: a run up to 10 % longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days, or a shorter run): adjusted hazard rate ratio 1.64 (95 % CI 1.31 to 2.05) for >10-30 % (81 overuse injuries), 1.52 (1.16 to 2.00) for >30-100 % (56) and 2.28 (1.50 to 3.48) for >100 % (24). These are relative rates, not absolute risks. Weekly load: spikes by the acute:chronic ratio went with a *lower* injury rate and the week-to-week ratio showed *no* relationship. Limits the authors state: self-reported injuries, distance as the only load measure, no subgroup analysis (previous injury, weekly mileage, age and BMI were not examined as modifiers), observational design: randomised trials are needed to show that staying under 10 % prevents injury. **So:** the single-run flag is research (observational, mostly experienced adult runners); using it for novices, rides or swims is an extrapolation (gap); a week-over-week jump flag has **no support in the largest cohort** and stays a practice prompt only.
- **Fokkema 2020 (Scand J Med Sci Sports; full text read).** Questionnaires before and after the event: 556 half-marathon and 441 marathon recreational runners, training and injuries self-reported. In the half marathon >32 km/week (beta -4.19, 95 % CI -6.52 to -1.85) and a longest run >21 km went with faster finishes; in the marathon <40 km/week went with slower (beta 6.33) and >65 km/week with faster finishes (beta -14.09), and a longest run <25 km with slower (beta 13.44); no association with injuries was found. Limits: self-report; 14.7 % of the half-marathon group also ran the marathon; many runners were excluded and the excluded were younger, heavier and less experienced (groups with higher injury risk), which may bias the injury result; speed ("talent") may confound; low-volume high-intensity plans were not tested. **So:** "no association found" is not proof that high volume is safe; the long-run and volume caps in the plans stay practice informed by this study.

## Triathlon, cycling and swimming injury evidence (read 2026-11-17)
- **Guevara 2024 (systematic review of injury and illness in short-course triathletes; FULL TEXT read, results and discussion).** 42 articles (26 cohort studies, 1 case-control, 15 case reports) covering 9,824 triathletes. The most common problems were lower-limb overuse injuries mainly from running, gastrointestinal illness and altered cardiac function attributed to contaminated water and thermal strain, and respiratory infections. Between 2 % and 15 % of competitors were injured during competition (15 % at the 2012 Olympics). Limits the authors state: inconsistent injury and illness definitions, few high-quality prospective studies, and surveillance that covers competition far more than training. **So:** running is where short-course triathletes get hurt most, which is why brick runs stay short, the run is capped, and the plan asks about leg pain; research (low-to-moderate quality observational evidence).
- **Rhind 2022 (systematic review of musculoskeletal injuries in long-distance triathletes; abstract and design read).** 6 studies; overuse injury incidence 37-91 %, acute injury 24-27 %; knee and spine most affected; running and cycling the most affected disciplines; elite athletes had less overuse injury (37 %); study quality poor (one study met more than half of the quality questions; two were prospective). **So:** the high overuse burden in long-distance triathlon is consistent with Andersen 2013, but the numbers are imprecise.
- **Visentini 2022 (systematic review of overuse injury in cyclists; abstract read, paywalled).** 18 studies, only 3 at low risk of bias; moderate evidence of a relationship between training load and symptoms; moderate evidence of **no** relationship between overuse pain and many measures traditionally used in bike fitting; conflicting evidence for seat height. **So:** control load (research, moderate); do not present bike-fit rules as injury prevention (no strong evidence).
- **Swimmer's shoulder (four papers, abstracts read; paywalled).** McKenzie 2023 (systematic review, 22 studies): no strong evidence for or against any of 80 variables; moderate evidence that competitive level and shoulder muscle recruitment patterns are associated with pain; moderate evidence **against** an association for range of motion, training frequency, stroke specialty, height, weight, sex and age. Hill 2015 (critical systematic review of 29 articles): the level of certainty for risk factors was low. Walker 2012 (12-month cohort, 74 swimmers aged 11 to 27): 38 % reported shoulder pain that interfered with training and 23 % a significant injury lasting 2 weeks or more; both high and low external rotation range went with higher risk (very wide confidence intervals). Tate 2012 (cross-sectional, 236 female swimmers): about one in five reported shoulder pain or disability in every age group. **So:** shoulder pain is common in swimmers, but **no risk-factor rule is supported**; the skills ask about shoulder pain, treat pain that interferes as a stop sign, and raise swim volume gradually (practice).
- **Gaps (no source read):** a swim volume or spike rule, a ride spike rule, and injury rules for duathlon. The 10 % single-run rule remains research for running only (Frandsen 2025).

## Chronic and acute load (CTL, ATL, TSB): context only (added 2026-11-17)
- **What the scripts show.** Garmin's own per-activity training load (`activityTrainingLoad`, from the watch) summed per day, then smoothed over 42 days (chronic load, CTL) and 7 days (acute load, ATL); TSB = CTL - ATL. Both averages start at the mean daily load of the first 28 days of the window. Shown in `load_check.py` and the weekly and monthly reports. **No flag, no band, no injury claim comes from it.**
- **Label: practice.** The 42-day and 7-day constants are a coaching convention from the fitness-fatigue idea. No paper read here validates them, or the TSB, as a measure of form or of injury risk. Garmin's load figure is the watch's own estimate; its accuracy was not checked here.
- **Jones 2017 (systematic review of longitudinal studies, 68 studies of injury and illness in athletes; FULL TEXT read, abstract, conclusions and limitations).** Athletes were at increased risk of injury or illness during periods of training-load intensification and of accumulated load; **acute change in training load showed a clear positive relationship with injury**, while other load and fatigue measures gave mixed associations; the authors name inconsistent terminology and a lack of work on individual characteristics as limits, and the search ran to August 2015. **So:** supports watching sudden changes in load (research, observational), not any specific CTL, ATL or TSB value.
- **Why there is no acute:chronic ratio.** Jones 2017 said ratios such as ACWR may predict better than acute load alone, but Impellizzeri 2020 (critical review, abstract) found no evidence supporting ACWR for managing load or recommending thresholds, and Frandsen 2025 (full text) found no week-to-week relationship in the largest running cohort. The scripts therefore compute neither the ratio nor a pass/fail band.
- **Gaps (no source read):** a validated form of the 42/7 constants for recreational endurance athletes; whether TSB tracks performance or readiness; how Garmin's load compares with other load measures.
