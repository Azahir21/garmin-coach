# Evidence map: garmin-planner

Strength labels: **research** (a paper read says this, with its limits), **mixed**, **practice** (coaching convention, no verified source), **gap** (nothing verified). Papers are in `library.md`; "full" = the full text was read, "abstract" = only the abstract was read, so only what it says is used.

| Rule in the plan | Label | Paper and what it actually found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start with mostly easy running; base before pace | research (small trials) | Zinner 2018 (abstract): in 42 recreational runners, high-intensity, low-intensity and polarized mesocycles all improved 5000 m time and VO2peak; running economy changed only with the low-intensity block; non-response was more likely with the high-intensity and polarized blocks. Festa 2019 (full): 38 recreational runners, 8 weeks, polarized (77/3/20) vs focused (40/50/10) gave similar gains (velocity at VO2max +3.2 vs +4.0 %, 2 km speed +3.5 vs +3.0 %). | 8-week trials, recreational runners with a VO2peak of about 45; not proof that low-intensity is best for this athlete. |
| After the base, keep high-intensity time at about 20 % or less of weekly time | mixed | Neal 2013 (abstract): 12 trained cyclists, 6 weeks of 80/0/20 vs 57/43/0: polarized gave larger gains in peak power (+8 vs +3 %), lactate threshold (+9 vs +2 %) and high-intensity capacity. Stoggl 2014 (full) and Stoggl & Sperlich 2015 (full review): randomised studies tend to favour polarized; observational data from elite athletes mostly show a pyramidal pattern. Festa 2019: a focused model gave similar gains in recreational runners. | Small samples; "trained cyclists" are fitter than this athlete. No paper read says which split is best for him. The 20 % is the share used in those trials. |
| Weekly volume rises by at most 10 % between build weeks | practice (conservative) | Buist 2008 (abstract): 532 novice runners, a 13-week graded programme built on the 10 % rule did not reduce running injuries (20.8 % vs 20.3 %). Nielsen 2014 (abstract): in 874 novice runners, progressing weekly distance by more than 30 % over two weeks vs less than 10 % was associated with distance-related injuries (HR 1.59, 95 % CI 0.96-2.66, P = .07); no significant difference overall; exploratory. | The 10 % rule is **not shown** to prevent injury; the data suggest a large jump is the worry. We use 10 % as a cautious step, not as proof. |
| Long run at most +10 % over the longest run of the last 30 days | research (observational) | Frandsen 2025 (full): in a 5200-person cohort, a single run longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days was associated with higher injury risk, rising with the size of the jump; the reference category was up to a 10 % increase. | Association, runs only. Applied to rides as an extrapolation (gap for cycling). |
| Reset week (x0.70) and deload week (x0.75) | practice | No paper read. Triggered by the athlete's own data: readiness RED, 14 days without rest, a weekly jump above 20 % (also a practice cut-off), an active injury. | Factors are convention. |
| Easy effort by heart-rate ceiling plus the talk test | research (review) | Reed & Pipe 2014 (abstract): comfortable speech is likely possible below the ventilatory or lactate threshold and not above it; the test works across walking, jogging and cycling. | HR ceilings come from the watch zones (method unconfirmed). |
| Strength twice a week, heavy-ish, low volume | research for the type, practice for exercises | Ronnestad & Mujika 2014 (abstract): heavy or explosive strength added to endurance training improved running economy; heavy strength is recommended for cycling economy and has the most compelling evidence of added cycling performance. Blagrove 2018 (full): strength training in distance runners and the physiological determinants of performance. | Exercise lists, sets and loads are practice. |
| Home strength with about 19 kg of plates, a band and the floor | practice (evidence gap) | The trials behind the strength rows above used heavy or explosive loading. No paper read tests light-load, single-leg, slow-tempo substitutes in endurance athletes. | Expect a smaller effect than in the trials; progress by tempo, pauses, reps and band tension. If heavier weights become available, the plan should move to heavier sets. |
| Strength at least 3 h after cardio; lower-body work not the evening before a key session | research (gap) + practice | Schumann 2022 (full): concurrent training does not blunt strength or size gains; Eddens 2018 (full): intra-session order and the gap matter for the interference effect. The 18-hour window is practice. | |
| Optional gate: a whole session run with no walking (a goal a user can set; only a lock if `requires_gate` is true) | the user's own rule | Walking is detected from FIT cadence (the cut-off, 125 steps/min for 15 s, is an engineering threshold from one runner's files, not a published one). | Change the criteria in the goal if it does not fit. |
| 3 km time trial to anchor paces; `paces.py` zones and predictions | practice (upstream tool) | The calculator is the MIT `running-coach` script (Riegel-type equivalence). Vickers 2016 (full, in `garmin-coach`) covers marathon prediction from training. | Riegel-type prediction was not re-verified here; a time trial from an unfit state can mislead. |
| Cycling: how much more power a faster average speed needs | research for the model form, assumption for numbers | Martin 1998 (abstract): the power model predicted measured power well. | CdA, rolling resistance and mass are assumptions; speed on rides depends on wind and stops. |
| Flat route = at most 8 m of climbing per km (raised from 5 after a user said their routes have small climbs) | practice | None. | Barometric climb from the watch; wind and traffic are not captured. |
| Cooler start for long rides | research (heat skill) | El Helou 2012 (full, in `garmin-heat-weather`): air temperature is the dominant weather factor in marathon pace. | Marathon data, not rides. |

| Session layouts (warm-up + steady block + cool-down; easy, long and short run sizes) | practice (user settings) | Defaults come from common coaching layouts; the user's own sizes are read from athlete.json `preferences`. | No paper read for the exact minutes. |
| Optional monthly MAF-style test (a fixed heart rate, equal laps, same course; settings in `tests.maf`) | practice | Maffetone & Laursen 2020 (full text, CC BY): the 180-age formula is not validated; the concept of pace at a fixed heart rate is supported (Vesterinen 2016/2017). | The user chooses the heart rate; the planner does not compute it for them. |
| HR targets: the watch's own zone when one zone covers the band, else custom bpm; warm-up and cool-down have no alert | practice | Garmin's manual says zones can be based on max HR, heart-rate reserve or LTHR but prints no percentages; the athlete's own watch screens give them (running %max, cycling %LTHR on 2026-11-16). | Zone targets resolve on the watch from the current zone set: a mismatch between `athlete.json hr.zone_sets` and the watch gives wrong bpm. The no-data estimate (median average HR of steady sessions) is not validated. |
| Tempo run: a steady block at a hard-but-controlled effort (block length and heart-rate band are user settings) | practice | A coaching convention, not a study. | Hard share <= 20% of the week (the share used in Neal 2013, Festa 2019, Stoggl 2014). On by default; locked per user only by their own data. It replaces an easy run, so it adds no volume. |
| Interval run: short hard repeats with rests (reps, length and band are user settings) | practice | None. | On by default, locked per user by the same rules as the tempo run. |
| Climb ride: a total-ascent target reached by repeating a short climb (settings in `preferences.climb`) | practice (evidence gap) | No study read on climbing for recreational cyclists. | Seated, steady, easy gears; the bike advice comes from `preferences.climb.bike_hint`. |
| Garmin workout file format (steps, targets, repeats) | facts from Garmin's FIT profile and "Workout Files" article | File order file_id, workout, workout_step; repeat step stores the first step index and the repeat count; HR range = bpm + 100; time in ms, distance in cm, speed in mm/s. | Name limits and device loading are not stated by Garmin; files are NOT tried on a real watch. |
| LTHR from the watch (auto-detect or guided test) is an estimate, usable as a rough anchor, not a lab value | research (small studies) | Lu 2025: 100 recreational runners, Garmin Forerunner 265 (n = 23) LT HR vs lab Dmax-mod: mean absolute error 11.4 bpm (7.2 %), r = 0.67, only 65 % of tests produced a value; its pace estimate was too high. Heiber 2024: Fenix 7 (n = 26, chest strap needed) LT HR 174 vs 177 bpm in a blood-lactate field test (-1.7 %, not significant) but the watch's spread was narrower (SD 4.6 vs 10 bpm) and its pace was 12 % too low. Systematic review 2025 (13 studies, mostly Garmin + chest strap + Firstbeat): LT estimates validated in only three studies. | Small samples, mostly young recreational runners, no study of this athlete's watch model; results differ in direction between studies. A fixed %max HR anchor with an ESTIMATED max HR is also poor (165 runners: 7-8 bpm error at LT1, 5-7 bpm at LT2 versus 5-7 and 3-5 with measured max). A true individual threshold needs blood lactate or gas exchange; field proxies (talk test, speed-HR slope-variation point) correlate but are not substitutes for the criterion in an individual (narrative review 2026). |

## Not supported / not said
- "The 10 % rule prevents injuries": contradicted by the randomised trial (Buist 2008).
- "Polarized training is always better": mixed (see above).
- Any date or time for reaching a goal (a race time, a ride speed): no paper read gives a rate of improvement for an individual. Progress is checked with scripts.
- Interval formats (strides, 5 x 3 min), exercise selection and loads: practice.
