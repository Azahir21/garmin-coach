# Evidence map: rampwise-analyze

"Verified" = the abstract or full text was read (see `library.md`). Strength: **research** = trial, cohort, meta-analysis or systematic review; **practice** = convention without a study in this library; **gap** = unsupported.
The thresholds live in `scripts/analysis.py`. This skill raises flags to look closer. It does not predict injuries: no paper read here gives a rule that predicts injury in one person.

## Not supported (do not claim)
- That any flag predicts an injury in this person. They are associations from groups.
- That staying under 10 % per week prevents injury (Buist 2008 found no effect).
- That watch data detects tendon, bone or joint problems. Only the user can report pain.
- That a low heart-rate variability or short sleep "causes" an injury: no paper read shows that in adults.
- Cycling rules: no study here tests a spike rule for rides.

## Full-text reading notes (read in full 2026-11-18)
What the full texts add to the rows above: sample, effect, and the limits the authors state. Where a row above says more than this, this section wins.

- **Frandsen 2025 (BJSM; full text read).** Design: the Garmin-RUNSAFE prospective cohort, 18 months, 5,205 adult runners (mean age 45.8, 22 % women, median 9.5 years of running), recruited through Garmin newsletters and European running clubs; distance came from Garmin devices, injuries from a weekly self-report; 1,820 runners (35 %) reported an injury, 1,311 of them overuse. Single-session result (reference: a run up to 10 % longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days, or a shorter run): adjusted hazard rate ratio 1.64 (95 % CI 1.31 to 2.05) for >10-30 % (81 overuse injuries), 1.52 (1.16 to 2.00) for >30-100 % (56) and 2.28 (1.50 to 3.48) for >100 % (24). These are relative rates, not absolute risks. Weekly load: spikes by the acute:chronic ratio went with a *lower* injury rate and the week-to-week ratio showed *no* relationship. Limits the authors state: self-reported injuries, distance as the only load measure, no subgroup analysis (previous injury, weekly mileage, age and BMI were not examined as modifiers), observational design: randomised trials are needed to show that staying under 10 % prevents injury. **So:** the single-run flag is research (observational, mostly experienced adult runners); using it for novices, rides or swims is an extrapolation (gap); a week-over-week jump flag has **no support in the largest cohort** and stays a practice prompt only.
- **Craven 2022 (Sports Med; full text read).** Meta-analysis of 69 publications and 227 outcomes, 89 % of participants male; sleep loss was defined as 6 hours or less in 24 hours. Mean change in performance -7.56 % (95 % CI -11.9 to -3.13), heterogeneity very high (I2 98.1 %). Consistent harm only after total deprivation and late restriction (waking early), not early restriction; about 0.4 % lower performance per extra hour awake before the task; tasks in the evening were consistently affected, morning tasks largely not; 98 % of outcomes measured sleep quantity only; just 8 of 227 outcomes were after 18:00. **So:** the 6-hour line matches the paper's own definition but is a readiness convention; the evidence is about performance, not injury, mostly men, and mostly single nights; the "evening session needs recovery" rule rests on few late-day outcomes.
- **Buchheit 2014 (Front Physiol; full text read).** A narrative review, not a trial. It favours 5-minute resting heart rate and time-domain heart-rate variability (rMSSD) recorded near-daily, plus submaximal exercise heart rate (30-60 s), interpreted against the measure's error and the smallest important change and the training context; resting HRV at least 3-4 days a week for chronic responses; and it warns that heart rate measures cannot inform on all aspects of wellness, fatigue and performance. **So:** the watch's overnight HRV is not the standardised morning 5-minute measure the paper describes, and the red/amber/green thresholds are practice; flags are prompts, read against the user's own baseline.
